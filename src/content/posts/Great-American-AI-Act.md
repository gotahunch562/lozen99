Congressman Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Congresswoman Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a discussion draft of the Great American AI Act. Although the draft is framed around frontier AI governance, its board-facing significance is evidence, it discusses: what must be documented, verified, reported, audited, and measured. Sections 101 through 123 build a documentation-and-verification architecture for large frontier developers: written frameworks, pre-deployment risk reports, critical safety incident filings, and independent audits. Sections 241 through 257 build a parallel evidence architecture for workforce impact: AI-attributed layoff disclosures, occupational forecasts, and federal survey data on AI adoption. Both halves of the bill are asking the same underlying question from opposite directions:  **what happened, and can it be proven?** That is one of the questions the [Name Standard℠ was built to ask](/disclosure-independent-governance/), and it sits directly underneath this bill's compliance architecture rather than inside it.

## Why this matters to boards

Boards sitting above a large frontier developer, or above a company whose workforce is being reshaped by AI adoption, will increasingly be handed the outputs this bill requires: a frontier AI framework to approve, an [Independent Verification Organization (IVO) oversight model](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/2fshFN09g0WvUrdtb_BA0g.pdf) to understand, a critical safety incident report to respond to, and a WARN Act disclosure to oversee or review as part of workforce-risk reporting. 

## Mapping the Name Standard℠ to the GAAIA discussion draft


| Name Standard℠ pillar | GAAIA provision | What the bill requires |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Time allocation** | Frontier AI framework review (Sec. 111) | Large frontier developers must write, implement, and publicly post a framework covering catastrophic risk thresholds, model weight cybersecurity, and deployment decisions a document someone must actually have allocated time to review, not simply approve on submission. |
| **Review capacity** | IVO audits and catastrophic-risk mitigation (Sec. 112) | CAISI-licensed Independent Verification Organizations audit whether a developer's framework achieves "acceptable levels of catastrophic risk mitigation." The audit itself is evidence of review capacity but an outsourced audit answers a different question than whether the board's own reviewers had the capacity to engage with what the audit found. |
| **Information access** | Deployment reports and risk assessments (Sec. 111) | Pre-deployment reports must disclose intended use, restrictions, risk assessments, and mitigation steps while permitting redactions for trade secrets, cybersecurity, or national security. Redaction authority means the board's information access can be narrower than the developer's own. |
| **Documentation infrastructure** | Model documentation templates (Sec. 123) | NIST's pilot template standardizes what gets documented: model name, developer identity, training data cutoff, terms of service. This is infrastructure for capturing evidence, not infrastructure for assigning who is accountable for what the evidence shows. |
| **Formal right of refusal** | Internal pause, restriction, remediation, or rejection authority | No section of the discussion draft establishes this. Sec. 113's whistleblower protections address retaliation *after* someone reports a violation externally, they are not the same as a named internal actor holding standing authority to pause, restrict, or reject a deployment before it happens. |


## The Lozen Advisory view

Four of the five pillars have a clear institutional home in [The Great American Artificial Intelligence Act.](https://obernolte.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/obernolte.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/the-great-american-ai-act-discussion-draft-website-compressed-compressed.pdf) The fifth does not, and it is the pillar the other four depend on for meaning. A framework can be written, reviewed, and audited; a deployment report can be filed with full information access; a documentation template can be completed correctly, and an organization can still lack anyone with the standing authority to say no before deployment, rather than report a violation after the fact. Sec. 113 protects the person who speaks up after something has already gone wrong. It does not create the role that could have stopped it going wrong in the first place. That gap is not a flaw unique to this bill, it is the default condition of disclosure-dependent governance, and it is precisely why **documentation volume and accountability are not the same asset,** even when a bill this detailed makes it easy to mistake one for the other.
