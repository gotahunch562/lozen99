## The SEC Did Not Just Find AI Washing

The Presto Automation enforcement action is not only an AI-washing case. It is a disclosure-control case about ownership, human intervention, and whether a company can describe an AI product as autonomous when human beings remain embedded in the operating model.

On January 14, 2025, the Securities and Exchange Commission announced settled charges against Presto Automation, Inc., a restaurant-technology company formerly listed on Nasdaq under the ticker symbol "PRST," for making materially false and misleading statements about Presto Voice, its flagship AI product for drive-thru order taking.

The SEC found that Presto made false and misleading statements in SEC filings and public statements from November 2021 through May 2023. The company consented to a cease-and-desist order. The SEC did not impose civil penalties, citing Presto's voluntary cooperation and remedial efforts, including corrective disclosures made after the Commission began its investigation.

The enforcement signal is direct: public-company AI claims now require disclosure controls that can verify the difference between what the product is called, what the company says it does, and what humans are still doing behind it.

---

## Presto Wrote Out the Human

The SEC order identifies two false claims and a third structural failure.

**False claim one:** From November 2021 to September 2022, Presto described Presto Voice as "our technology" and "Presto's technology" while failing to disclose that every commercially deployed unit was powered by technology owned and operated by Hi Auto, a third-party supplier.

**False claim two:** After deploying its own proprietary technology in September 2022, Presto claimed Presto Voice "eliminates human order taking." The original version required human agent intervention on every order. A more advanced version, piloted June 2023, still required human intervention approximately 70 percent of the time. The agents were located primarily in the Philippines and India.

**Third violation:** From September 2022 through December 2024, Presto had no disclosure controls or procedures. No one at the company was formally responsible for ensuring that its Commission filings were accurate.

*Full findings: [SEC Order, File No. 3-22413 (Jan. 14, 2025)](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2025/33-11352.pdf)*

## The Numbers Were Also Misleading

The SEC order contains a specific finding about how Presto reported its performance metrics that deserves close attention.

From November 2021 through January 2023, Presto used the terms "automated order completion" rate and "non-intervention" rate in public filings and investor presentations. The company represented that Presto Voice had achieved rates of 95 to 99 percent at its largest customer's restaurants, and claimed "non-intervention" rates consistently above 95 percent.

The SEC found that those figures were misleading in two distinct ways.

First, the rates Presto reported did not measure what investors would reasonably have understood them to measure. They measured orders completed without restaurant staff intervention — meaning on-site workers at the restaurant were not involved. They did not measure orders completed without any human intervention. The offshore agents in the Philippines and India entering those orders were not counted as interventions. The metric was constructed to make the system appear far more autonomous than it actually was.

Second, across all restaurants powered by Presto's proprietary technology, the actual non-intervention rate — meaning orders completed without any human involvement — averaged 85 percent. Presto achieved non-intervention rates at or above 95 percent only at certain specific restaurant locations. The headline figures Presto was reporting to investors bore no relationship to the system-wide reality of the product's autonomous capability.

The order notes that Presto's own executives understood the terminology was likely to mislead. In October 2022, a senior Presto executive messaged a group of other executives that the company should not refer to "automation rate with customers because it infers no supervision which isn't true." A subsequent internal discussion about which terminology to use — "automated completion rate," "intervention rate," or other alternatives — produced no corrective action in investor-facing materials.

In January 2023, another Presto executive raised concerns internally that the company was "telling investors Presto AI is running 95%+ accuracy without disclosing AI is doing NONE of the work and all orders are processed by humans."

Similar concerns were raised by several other senior executives. No corrective disclosure was made until after the Commission's investigation began.

## Why This Is a Name Standard℠ Case

This is exactly the kind of failure the Name Standard℠ is designed to surface.

The Name Standard℠ asks a practical governance question: when AI-assisted work moves through a company's name, a product's name, an executive's name, or a human's professional authority, has the accountable human contribution been preserved, verified, and disclosed where material?

In the Presto case, the named product carried the automation claim. Presto Voice was presented as a system that eliminated human order taking. The SEC found that humans were not only involved — for most of the relevant period, they were required for every order. And internally, Presto's own executives knew that the claim was false while the company continued making it.

What Presto did was not a failure to understand its own product. It was a decision — documented in internal messages, reflected in metric design, and embedded in filing language — to describe an AI-human hybrid system as if it were a fully autonomous one. The company constructed a market-facing account of Presto Voice that removed the human from view while the human remained essential to the system's operation.

The Name Standard℠ forces that distinction into view. It does not ask whether AI is impressive. It asks whose labor, judgment, supervision, correction, or verification is still carrying the output — and whether that has been disclosed.

A company can reduce on-site labor without eliminating human labor. It can remove the visible worker from the customer interface while relying on remote human workers, contractors, vendors, reviewers, correctors, or escalation teams elsewhere in the workflow. That is not the elimination of human intervention. It is the relocation of human intervention.

In Presto's case, the relocation was specific and operational: drive-thru orders were being completed by human agents in the Philippines and India, not by the AI system Presto was describing to investors. The offshore workers were the bridge between what the product could do and what the company represented the product was doing.

## The Governance Signal for Boards

For boards, audit committees, disclosure teams, and AI governance leaders, the Presto case should not be read narrowly.

The issue is not whether a company uses AI. The issue is whether the company's AI claims match the actual allocation of work, accountability, ownership, and human intervention.

When a company says an AI system eliminates human work, that statement shapes how investors understand product maturity, scalability, labor costs, customer adoption, margins, and competitive advantage. If the underlying product still depends on substantial human intervention — particularly offshore human intervention that is invisible in the customer-facing account — the automation claim may create a materially misleading picture of the company's technology and operating model.

The Presto order also demonstrates that this is not merely a technical accuracy problem. The company's own executives flagged the terminology as misleading in real time. The issue persisted because there was no formal process — no one formally responsible — for ensuring that investor disclosures matched operational reality. The third violation in the order, the disclosure controls failure, is the governance mechanism that allowed everything else to continue.

The board-level questions are direct:

Does the company actually own or control the AI technology it describes as proprietary, and is the extent of third-party reliance disclosed?

Does the company distinguish between eliminating on-site labor and eliminating human intervention, including remote, offshore, or contracted human support?

Do automation metrics separate AI-completed work from work completed with human agent involvement, and is the basis for those metrics disclosed?

Are there internal communications — from executives, product teams, or engineering — raising concerns about the accuracy of public AI claims, and what is the formal path for those concerns to reach the disclosure process?

Can the disclosure controls confirm that public AI claims have been reconciled against the actual workflow?

These are not only technical questions. They are disclosure-control questions, and they are board evidence questions.

AI governance cannot stop at model documentation, vendor review, or technical accuracy testing. A company also needs a mechanism — a formal, documented, accountable mechanism — to verify whether public claims about AI ownership, AI capability, AI autonomy, and AI-driven labor substitution match operational reality. The absence of that mechanism is itself an Exchange Act violation.

## The Human Was Still There

The Presto case is powerful because the enforcement theory returns the human to the center of the AI story.

The company claimed AI eliminated the need for human order taking.

The SEC found that for most of the relevant period, humans were required for every order — and that Presto's own executives knew it.

That is the Name Standard℠ argument in enforcement form.

When AI carries a company's name, the human layer cannot be treated as invisible if that human layer is still necessary to make the system function. The offshore agent in Manila or Bangalore entering the drive-thru order is not an implementation detail. If people are still reviewing, correcting, completing, approving, escalating, or taking the order — if the system cannot function without them — the company has not eliminated human intervention. It has only changed where the human sits and whether investors can see them.

Presto wrote the human out.

The SEC wrote them back in.
