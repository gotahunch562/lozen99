Employers making decisions about menopause at work support are working from available data. Accommodation requests, leave utilization, healthcare benefit engagement, and manager escalations form the basis for what most organizations can measure. What prior articles establish at the level of policy and law appears here at the level of organizational data. If S7495 is a study of the legislative policy gap, the following analysis is a study of the organizational data gap. The visibility constraint is the same: the system cannot measure what it has not been designed to collect.

It reflects what employees report. It does not reflect what they do not.

A global survey by Korn Ferry Institute in partnership with Vira Health, covering more than 8,000 women, makes the workforce impact precise. 47% of women reported that perimenopause and menopause-related symptoms disrupted their work performance, with 40% experiencing six or more distinct symptoms affecting their effectiveness. Up to 40% reported missing from a few days to a week of work in any given month. 28% have quit or considered quitting due to symptoms. Yet only 26% reported having formal workplace policies or programs addressing menopause. Only 42% had spoken with their line manager about their experiences, 37% with HR, and only 27% with senior leadership. The majority did not talk, or were not willing to talk, with the people managing them. These are not outlier responses. They describe a significant share of the workforce: employees who are currently performing, currently impacted, and currently not appearing in the data employers use to assess need.

A separate Maven Clinic survey shows that 76% of millennials in perimenopause or menopause say they are more likely to stay at jobs with menopause benefits. That figure measures willingness to disclose from a position where employer-provided benefits are still a primary form of leverage. The willingness to disclose correlates with how much a woman has to lose from being perceived as less than fully operational. Early-career employees making that statement are making a rational calculation from where they sit. The woman who has built the leverage, who holds the client relationship, who is being evaluated for the next stage of her career, has made a different calculation. The data aggregates across fundamentally different risk positions. It does not distinguish between the employee for whom disclosure is low-cost and the one for whom it is career-limiting.

## The 73-Point Perception Gap: Why Disclosure Is a Flawed Metric

The 73-point gap is not a communication failure; it is a measurement architecture failure.

A 2023 study conducted by Bank of America in partnership with the National Menopause Foundation, surveying 2,000 employed women and 500 HR benefit managers at companies with 1,000 or more employees, makes the mechanism explicit. 58% of peri- and post-menopausal women reported they do not feel comfortable discussing symptoms at work, with 80% describing the topic as too personal to raise. The reasons they gave are not abstract: 32% feared being perceived as old, 28% found it embarrassing to discuss their body, 23% did not want peers to treat them differently, and 18% feared losing the respect of male colleagues. That is not stigma in the abstract. That is a documented professional risk calculation that produces non-participation.

The Bank of America study also reveals the measurement architecture failure at its most precise. 76% of HR benefit managers said they discuss menopause with employees regularly or sometimes. Only 3% of peri- and post-menopausal employees said they had talked to HR about it. That 73-point gap is not a communication failure. It is a measurement system capturing its own activity and mistaking it for impact. The primary reason employers reported not offering menopause benefits was that employees had not asked for them. The absence of requests became the justification for inaction.

The [LiveCareer Working Through Menopause Report (2025)](https://www.livecareer.com/resources/working-through-menopause-report), surveying nearly 900 employed women in the United States, confirms the pattern at the individual level. 97% reported feeling pressured to hide or downplay their symptoms at work. Among those who chose not to speak about menopause at work, 61% cited company culture as the reason, 61% feared being judged, and 18% said they did not trust HR. Nearly one in three had considered changing jobs, reducing their role, or cutting back on hours due to symptoms, a consideration-of-exit that generated no organizational signal and no record. Critically, 91% of women begin experiencing symptoms before age 50, the same period many step into senior management and leadership roles. The timing is not incidental. The period of highest physiological disruption overlaps with the period of highest career exposure. The woman navigating the highest-influence years of her career is the same woman the system was not built to hold. The pressure she feels to hide what she is managing is not a gap in psychological safety. It is an accurate assessment of what disclosure would cost her. That assessment is [Tacere, named from the inside](/blog/tacere-menopause-caregiving-attrition/).

## Structural Invisibility: The Failure of Administrative Measurement

The Michigan Women's Commission Menopause in the Workplace Survey makes the downstream cost visible. Fewer than one in five respondents disclosed their menopause status at work. Only 8% expressed satisfaction with the menopause-related resources available at their workplace. When survey data was extrapolated to the Michigan workforce, the estimated annual loss from employees leaving due to menopause symptoms was 16,500 women. That is not a projection from a model. It is a count from a state-funded, independently reviewed survey conducted across 652 respondents.

The gap between reported impact and total impact is not a data quality problem but a structural condition of how the data is generated. Employers see utilization, but they do not see the decision not to utilize. That decision is the data point the system was never designed to collect.

## A Tale of Two Exits: Modeling the Unquantified Financial Loss

Two examples illustrate how this plays out. The financial outcome is the same in both.

In the first, a senior employee exits. No prior signals. Performance reviews were strong. Attendance was unremarkable. Her exit is recorded as personal. Her replacement costs the organization up to 200% of her annual salary. Nothing in the data system indicated risk. The cost was real. The cause was never visible.

In the second, a senior employee exits. Prior to leaving: increased leave usage, no promotion over multiple cycles, a lateral role adjustment. Each signal was captured. None was connected to the others, and none was connected to a condition that affects roughly one in ten women who have gone through menopause and left a job because of it. Her exit is also recorded as personal. Same replacement cost. An estimated $2,100 in excess annual healthcare costs absorbed before she left. Fourteen-plus days of lost productivity in the year prior. The organization had more data in the second case. It reached the same conclusion.

Two different data failures. One never captured. One captured and misinterpreted. The financial loss is identical either way.

Michigan's Menopause Memorandum, New York's proposed legislation, and Virginia's proposed workforce study all operate within the same boundary. Legal frameworks capture what enters them. Benefit programs capture what is requested. Studies capture what is reported.

What is not entered, not requested, and not reported does not appear.

This is not a gap in compliance, however. An employer can meet every legal obligation and still be operating on partial data. Accommodation processes can function correctly. Documentation can be complete. Benefits can be available and underutilized. Each of these represents the system working as designed. The Bank of America data shows that employers can simultaneously believe they are having the conversation and be wrong by 73 points.

The question is whether the system, as designed, captures the full scope of impact on retention. The answer is no, and the difference is not marginal.

## The Mercer Framework Critique: Why Offerings Are Not Impact

Consider what the dominant employer advice model actually prescribes. Mercer, one of the world's largest HR consulting firms, advises employers to take nine steps toward menopause support:

Provide coverage

Offer telehealth

Allow flexible schedules

Accommodate shift workers

Offer cooling options

Prioritize education

Create a culture of openness

Include men in the conversation

Build peer support groups

Every one of those nine recommendations requires the woman to surface. She has to request the telehealth appointment. She has to use the cooling room. She has to join the peer group. She has to answer the anonymous survey. She has to say the word out loud. Nine recommendations. One hundred percent visibility required.

Not one recommendation addresses how to know whether any of it reached the employee who had already completed the risk calculation and decided that visibility costs more than it returns. The Mercer framework is a checklist of offerings. It is structurally identical to the legislative frameworks this series analyzes. Create the entitlement. Log the activity. Cite it as evidence. The model assumes visibility as the precondition for support. It has no mechanism for the population managing against visibility as a professional survival strategy.

The population managing against visibility is the population the entire model was built to miss.

## Why More Awareness Has Not Moved the Numbers

That population does not grow smaller as awareness increases. The volume of research on menopause in the workplace has grown steadily. The policy activity has accelerated. The employer frameworks have multiplied. By the logic of awareness-driven change, outcomes should be improving. The data does not show that.

The reasoning is not complicated.

More studies produce more awareness.

More awareness produces more frameworks.

More frameworks produce more support options. The engagement requirement does not change.

The population least likely to engage does not engage.

Outcomes for that segment do not improve.

Aggregate results appear stagnant or worsening.

Each step is real. None of them changes the variable that governs whether the support reaches the population that needs it most.

Every tool in the standard employer model activates on the same condition: the woman must produce an initiating act that creates a record. The frameworks have become more sophisticated. The condition has not changed.

The population least likely to engage is the population carrying the highest organizational cost. Senior executives, women in competitive and hierarchical environments, women being evaluated for the next stage of their careers have completed the risk calculation and decided against visibility. That calculation does not become less rational as awareness increases. It becomes more precise. The woman who has read the research knows exactly what the data shows about how disclosed conditions are perceived. Awareness, for her, is not an invitation to participate. It is additional information about the cost of participation.

The aggregate numbers reflect this. When the high-risk segment does not engage, its outcomes do not improve. Studies that measure utilization, program participation, or disclosure rates will show the programs reaching the population already willing to be reached. That population was never the measurement problem. The segment whose outcomes are not improving does not appear in the utilization data, because non-utilization produces no record. The studies that follow will measure the same gap and reach the same conclusion: more support is needed.

More support, built on the same model, will not reach the same woman it has not reached before. What it will reach is the same evidence trail: utilization logged, conversation documented, cooling room installed. The record will show the system working. The Power User Trap℠ shows what that looks like from inside the organization.

## The Power User Trap℠: When High Performance Masks High Risk

Recent executive cases confirm the structural argument. Both women had disclosed their conditions publicly. Both continued performing at full output through treatment and deteriorating health. Neither generated a signal in the organizational record that would have allowed the institution to act before the exit arrived. One had postponed medical tests and new therapies for her entire tenure to avoid missing a single day of work. The other had led through late-stage cancer treatment for a year and a half. In both cases, the record showed a fully functioning executive. The cost was absorbed entirely by the individual. The departure arrived as an announcement.

These are not cases of a system that failed to support its people. They are cases of a system that extracted maximum value from its most capable individuals while removing the conditions that would have triggered protection. The woman performing at full output does not register as someone who needs intervention. She registers as evidence that the system is working. The Power User Trap℠ is not a personal failing. It is what high pressure environments produce when capability becomes the disqualifying condition for support.

The [Korn Ferry and Vira Health survey data](https://www.kornferry.com/institute/korn-ferry-and-vira-health-survey-findings) confirms this at the career progression level. Senior executives reported that perimenopause and menopause-related symptoms had more than twice the effect on promotions, almost three times the effect on selection and hiring for a role, and more than three times the effect on bonuses compared to individual contributors. The women with the most to lose are experiencing the greatest career-level impact. The capability that made them senior executives concentrates the cost at the top of the organization, and concentrates the silence there too.

## The Policy Gap: What Organizational Data Cannot Measure

Invisible Attrition℠ describes this condition precisely: the unmeasured erosion of leadership and performance capacity that occurs before traditional retention metrics detect risk. The data is not missing. It was never created. It exists in the workforce as performance, as tenure, as client relationships held together by a woman who decided the collection condition would never be met. The system has no record for it because the system requires an initiating act she never produced.

This is not a framework that relates to the problem. It is the classification that governs it. Every accommodation request that was never filed, every leave day that was never taken, every exit interview that captured nothing because nothing was ever disclosed: those are not data gaps. They are instances of Invisible Attrition℠ operating exactly as the condition predicts. The pattern that Virginia's study will encounter, that New York's leave entitlements will not reach, that Michigan's data already confirmed: it is the same condition. The record will show compliance. It will show utilization. It will show that the conversation happened and the benefits were offered and the cooling room was installed. What the record will not show is the woman who calculated the cost of visibility, decided against it, and left. She is the data the system defined as the problem and then excluded from the record.
